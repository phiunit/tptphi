A School System Pauses Student AI

Example 1 of 4, teacher-facing. As reported by Chalkbeat, CNN, CBS New York and The 74, September 2, 2026. Summary in our own words; no figures.

The Story

New York City's public school system announced a new rule about generative AI, the kind of tool that writes text or makes images from a prompt. Under the rule, students from kindergarten through eighth grade will not use these tools for schoolwork this school year. Officials described the pause as lasting one school year. High schools get a different plan. Some high schools will try out approved AI tools in pilot programs. High school students will also take AI literacy lessons every year. The system is the largest in the country, so the rule reaches a very large number of students.

My 3-rule policy: Lesson first (Creative) · then approved tasks only, in class (Middle) · always say when AI helped (Loose).

Defend one rule, three sentences: The rule is: pass an AI literacy lesson before you may use the tool. It matters because a tool you don't understand can make up facts, and you would never know. We know this works because the story's own high school plan pairs the pilots with AI lessons every year: the tool and the training arrive together.

So what? I'm in this grade band. Whether I learn AI at school or only on my own depends on a rule like this one.

Why this frame: the story is a school system setting a rule, so the question is "what should the rule be", not "who was right". Frame 3 keeps students off the officeholders and on the grid, where every rule has a cost.

Musicians Sue an AI Music Company

Example 2 of 4, teacher-facing. As reported by Billboard, Rolling Stone, Variety and The Hollywood Reporter, August 31, 2026. Summary in our own words; every claim in the case is alleged.

The Story

A group of musicians filed a lawsuit against Suno, a company whose AI tool generates songs from a text prompt. They filed it as a proposed class action, which means they are asking to speak for many other musicians with the same complaint. The musicians allege that the tool can imitate a real artist's style and identity, meaning things like a voice or a name, without permission. A lawsuit is a list of claims that a court has not yet decided, so nothing in it is proven. The company will have its turn to answer in court. The case asks a question that comes up whenever AI learns from art: is copying a style the same as copying a song, or a singer?

Where I draw the line: Copying a style is fine when you make something new in it and anyone can hear it's yours. It crosses the line when you copy a person: a voice, a face or a name isn't a style anyone can learn. It's them.

So what? I make beats and post covers. Knowing which kind of copying I'm doing is the difference between a style I learned and a person I took.

Why this frame: a lawsuit about copying is a sorting problem before it is a verdict. Frame 7 lets students sort work, style and person without deciding the case, which no court has done either; every claim stays "alleged".

A Free Game-Data Site Changes Hands

Example 3 of 4, teacher-facing. As reported by Engadget, PC Gamer, Kotaku and heise, September 2, 2026. Summary in our own words; the owner is not named.

The Story

SteamDB is a free website that tracks information about games sold on Steam, such as price changes and how many people are playing. For years it was run by one person, mostly alone. That owner said running the site had led to burnout. The site was sold to the company that owns Nexus Mods, a large site where players share game modifications. The sale price was not made public. The new owner promised the site would stay free, with no ads and no paywall. Free sites still cost money to run, so fans asked the obvious question: if it stays free, who pays?

So what? Half the apps I use are free. Someone pays for every one of them, and I want to know which loss is the one I'm paying.

Why this frame: a free thing changing owners is Frame 4's whole question: if it's free, who pays? The funding grid turns one sale into five choices, and each choice has a loss the student has to name.

An AI Helper Can Act on Your Photos

Example 4 of 4, teacher-facing. As reported by 9to5Google, TechCrunch and PetaPixel, September 3, 2026. Summary in our own words; the kind of capability, not product tiers.

The Story

Google announced that Gemini, its AI assistant, can now act inside a person's Google Photos library. Reports described it as an agent, which means a tool that carries out tasks for you rather than only telling you how. A user can ask it to find the photos they describe and take actions on them, and it does the steps. Some actions in a photo library are easy to undo; others, like deleting or sharing, are not. A photo library holds a lot about a person: faces, places, dates. That is why a helper that can act on it needs clear limits, and why this story fits a permission slip.

Why this frame: an assistant that can act on a photo library is exactly what Frame 9 sorts: what may it do alone, what must it ask about, what never. The story supplies the helper; the student supplies the limits and the risky combo.
