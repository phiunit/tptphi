News Desk Day

A 45-minute routine that turns any news story into a thinking lesson. Pick a story, pick a frame, run the method. Ten frames, one skeleton, no prep, fully on paper. Bell-ringer version on page 2.

Objectives ISTE 1.3.bCCSS ELA RI.6.8CCSS ELA W.6.1CSTA 2-IC-20CCSS Math 7.RP.A.3

On any story they are handed, students can…

  • Trace a story's claims and judge which are backed by a second source. (Frames 1, 8; rubric row "evidence")
  • Compare who gains and who loses from a change in technology, make a call, defend it. (Frames 2, 3, 9; rubric "claim")
  • Turn a price change into a percent and a cost per unit, then into a plan. (Frames 5, 6; rubric "method followed")
  • Say plainly why the story matters to someone their age. (every frame's "So what?"; rubric "said plainly")

Pick the Frame — story type → frame

2A new tool, app or feature that changes how people do somethingThe TradeWho wins, who loses?

7An artist, game or brand saying something copied themCopy, Style, or Person?What got copied?
8A study, report or survey with a big number in itSource CheckWho's telling me this, and why?
9An AI helper that can act on someone's photos, messages or accountsPermission SlipWhat may it do on its own?
10A theft, hack, outage or lossRisk GridWhat would hurt most to lose?

Open — Headline Only (5 min)

Show or read the headline and nothing else. Ask: "What question does this headline make you want to ask?" Take three answers, then name the frame: "That's a Frame 2 question — who wins, who loses. That's today's frame."

If nobody has a question: read the first sentence aloud and ask which word they would check. That is a Frame 1 question. If every question is about a person: "We frame the change, not the person. What changed?"

Hand-out (say it): "Frames out. Top box first: the story, where you saw it, the date. Then we read."

Read — Mark It Up (10 min)

Students read the story once, printed, projected or read aloud by you. While they read: underline every claim they could check, circle any word they don't know. Then pairs retell the story to each other in one sentence each. That retell is the check that they actually read it.

If the story is above their reading level: read it aloud yourself, pausing after each paragraph for a one-sentence retell from a volunteer. If a student says "this is boring": "Frames don't care if it's boring. They care if it's true, fair, or expensive. Which one is this?"

Transition (say it): "Story down. Frame up. Method steps in order — number one first, and don't skip to the boxes."

Frame — Run the Method (20 min)

  1. Solo (12 min): students work the numbered steps in order and fill the frame. You circulate with the script for that frame (Teacher Guide p. 2–3): three quotable lines and what a strong answer looks like. Play the editor, not the answer key. Frames 3 and 6 carry the most writing: on those two, run solo 16 and pair-check 4 instead of 12 and 8.
    If a student stalls: "Which step are you on? Read it to me." The method is the help; you rarely need to add more. If a pair finishes early: they write the "So what?" for a different reader — a parent, a gamer, someone who runs a small shop.
    Transition: "Swap frames with your partner. Read their evidence, not their answer. Ask them one question they can't dodge."
  2. Pair-check (8 min): partners swap frames. Each reads the other's evidence and asks one question. Then frames go back and students fix one thing. The two-line "So what?" is written last, solo.
    If partners just say "looks good": "Point at the step they skipped. Every frame has one." If an odd number: one group of three; the third reads both frames.
    Transition: "Pens down. Frames face up. Share-out is one line each: your call, and the evidence behind it."

Share-out — One Line Each (10 min)

Round the room by pairs. Each pair gives one line: their verdict, call or pick, plus the one piece of evidence behind it. Tally on the board (RUN / FIX / KILL, WORTH IT / NOT, whatever the frame decides). Fifteen pairs at 35 seconds each fits 30 students in 9 minutes. Close: "The story changes every week. The method doesn't." Collect frames; the "So what?" lines are the exit ticket, scored with the rubric (Teacher Guide p. 4).

If two pairs disagree: score the evidence, not the answer. "Who named a source, followed a step, AND said it plainly? That pair wins the round." If time runs short: take one pair per tally column, then collect.

Bell-Ringer Variant — 25 min

Open 2 — headline only; one question from the room, you name the frame. Read 5 — you read the story aloud once; one volunteer retells it. Frame 15 — solo, no pair-check; steps in order, "So what?" last. Share-out 3 — three volunteers, one line each. Collect; same rubric.

If 15 minutes is not enough: Frames 3, 6 and 10 are the long ones. For a bell-ringer, run Frames 1, 2, 4, 7, 8 or 9.

Differentiation

  • Support: Start with Frames 1, 7 or 9 (fewest steps). Use the "Need a start?" sentence frames on Frames 3, 4, 7 and 9. Fill the story slot on the board together.
  • Extension: Run a second frame on the same story (if it fit two, this is the other one). One sentence: what did the second frame show that the first missed?
  • ELL: Read the story aloud twice; pairs retell before anyone writes. Method words (claim, source, verdict, percent) are defined in the steps on each frame. Draft the "So what?" in the strongest language first, then translate.
  • No-device classrooms: Built in — the routine runs on paper; students NAME the second source they would check. With devices: Frames 1 and 8 open that source live; Frames 5 and 6 check the math on a calculator.
