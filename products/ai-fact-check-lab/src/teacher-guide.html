Why This Lesson

A good AI job runs human → AI → human: a person starts it (the prompt) and a person ends it (the check). This lesson trains the second human job. Students leave with one idea: AI predicts likely words, and it doesn't always check them. So smooth is zero evidence of true. Lateral reading (leave the answer, confirm it in a second, independent source) is what professional fact-checkers do, and it works on AI answers, group-chat rumors, and feeds alike. Stands alone.

Answer Key — Warm-Up Paragraph (read-aloud)

Planted errorThe true fact
"The closest planet to the Sun is Venus, which is also the hottest."Mercury is closest to the Sun. Venus really is the hottest (its thick atmosphere traps heat). That half-truth is what makes it so slippery.
"Saturn… is the only planet in our solar system that has [rings]."Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune have faint rings too. Saturn's are just the most visible.

True claims (fine as C or S): eight planets; Mars is red from rusty iron dust on its surface; sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach Earth. Slide 6 shows the corrected paragraph for modeling.

When It Goes Sideways

A desk asks "is this one true?" → "That's a checkable claim. Where's your second source?" Play the editor, not the answer key, and keep deadline energy: "Presses roll in five!"

The trusting desk clears everything ("it sounded right") → send them back to step 1: "Did you underline the dates and names? Start there." Every draft has at least one wrong date, name, or place.

The paranoid desk kills everything ("it's all suspicious") → that's what MARK is for: "Which claim would hurt most if wrong? Check that one first." The goal is calibrated doubt, not panic.

A desk treats the Draft #3 quote as proof ("it's in quotation marks") → this is the hardest catch in the lesson, on purpose. Desk rule: a quote is a claim. No traceable source, no print. Expect to say it twice.

Discussion Questions (debrief, or the next day)

  • Most of the room voted to print the warm-up paragraph. What made it feel true? (Smooth sentences, confident tone, real-sounding details.)
  • Why is a fabricated quote more dangerous than a wrong date? (A date is easy to check; a quote borrows a real person's credibility.)
  • Marcus's ending was an opinion built on a false fact. Can an opinion be "wrong"? (It can be unsupported. When the evidence under it fails, so does the argument.)

Answer Key — The Three Drafts (grouped by draft)

C/S grading. Must be C: the planted errors and each ending. Any other C/S split earns credit if the desk can say why.

DraftC or S?What the desk should catchThe true fact
#1 PriyaMust be C: the 1959 landing date · "produces its own light" · the ending.
Either (true): Earth's only natural satellite · Armstrong walked first · 12 people by 1972.		"Astronauts first landed on the Moon in 1959"The first crewed landing was Apollo 11, in 1969. (Expect pushback: the uncrewed probe Luna 2 reached the Moon in 1959, but no astronauts landed.)
#1 Priya"the Moon… produces its own light"The Moon makes no light of its own. It reflects sunlight.
#1 PriyaEnding: "the brightest thing in the night sky"True claim, false reason. The Moon IS the brightest, but because it reflects sunlight. The argument breaks even though the ending is right. Verdict: FIX.
#2 TheoMust be C: Everest in the Andes · Australia's capital · the ending.
Either (true): the Pacific is the largest ocean · the Nile flows north through Egypt and is one of the world's longest rivers · seven continents (the model most US schools teach) · Asia is the largest.		"Mount Everest… rises in the Andes range"Everest is in the Himalayas, in Asia. The Andes are in South America.
#2 Theo"Australia's capital city is Sydney"The capital is Canberra, not Sydney — a classic mix-up.
#2 TheoEnding: "record-holders sit on three continents"Once Everest moves back to Asia, South America holds none of the passage's records, so the ending no longer follows. Verdict: FIX.
#3 MarcusMust be C: Edison and the telephone, 1876 · the Edison quote · the ending.
Either (true): the phonograph (1877) was the first machine to both record and play back sound · the Wright brothers' 1903 flights near Kitty Hawk, the widely accepted first (rival claims exist, so a desk that marks it C is doing its job right).		"Thomas Edison invented the telephone in 1876"Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876. Edison's fame rests on the phonograph and practical electric lighting.
#3 MarcusQuote: "The telephone is the finest of all my inventions."Fabricated: written for this exercise, with no source. AI can produce real-sounding quotes nobody said. No traceable source, no print.
#3 MarcusEnding: "the most important inventor of the 1800s"An opinion propped up by a false fact (Edison did not invent the telephone) and an invented quote. Verdict: KILL. The lead claim is false and its proof is invented, so the story goes. The phonograph and Wright facts are true and could start a new story.

Verdict Guidance + Exemplar Notes

Correct stamps. Draft #1: FIX (topic holds; correct the date and the light claim). Draft #2: FIX (correct the range and the capital). Draft #3: KILL (the lead claim and its quote both fail). A defended FIX earns partial credit. No draft earns RUN today. Tell the class: "No RUN today, and that's the point. These came straight from an AI helper, and nobody had checked them yet." Score the evidence, not the stamp.
Exemplar notes (proficient — each fits the four lines the student gets):
To Priya: "Wrong: astronauts landed in 1969, not 1959, and the Moon reflects light. Check NASA's Apollo 11 page. Ending stands, reason fails."
To Theo: "Wrong: Everest is in the Himalayas in Asia, and Australia's capital is Canberra. Check an atlas. The three-continent ending falls."
To Marcus: "Wrong: Bell invented the telephone, and that Edison quote has no source. Check an encyclopedia. Without the telephone and the quote, the ending has no support."
Partial: one error named, no second source. No credit: a stamp with no evidence.

Key — "Second source: where I'd check it" column

The ISTE 1.3.b item. Full credit: a specific KIND of source, independent of the draft and of the AI. Partial: "look it up online" or "Google it" with no site named. No credit: asked the AI again; the draft itself.

DraftAcceptable second sourcesWhat fails
#1 MoonA space-agency page (NASA) on Apollo 11 or the Moon; an encyclopedia entry "Moon"; a science textbook's astronomy chapter."Ask the AI if it's sure"; a video that repeats the claim; a quiz site that copied the claim.
#2 RecordsAn atlas or globe; an encyclopedia entry on Everest or Australia; a geography textbook; the Australian government's own site.A quiz site that copied the claim; a friend who "knows"; the draft itself.
#3 InventionsAn encyclopedia entry on Bell, Edison, or the telephone; a museum site (Smithsonian); a history textbook. For the quote: a quotation reference or a collection of Edison's writings. Nothing turns up, so it has no source."It's in quotation marks, so it's real"; asking the AI "did Edison say this?"

Exit Ticket Key

#1 — How can AI sound 100% sure and still be wrong? Full credit names the mechanism: AI predicts likely words, and it doesn't always check them. Confidence is a writing style, not evidence. Accept "it guesses what sounds right" or "it's a smooth talker, not a fact-checker." Partial credit: "it makes mistakes" with no why.

#2 — A real checkable claim + a second source that is NOT where they first saw or heard it. Full credit names a claim with a name, date, number, place, or quote in it AND a specific second source ("the NASA site, not the video that made the claim"; "a news site that didn't copy the first one"). Model: "A video said goldfish only remember things for three seconds. I'd check an encyclopedia entry on goldfish, not the video." Partial: "look it up online" or "Google it" with no site named. Push back: "Google shows you ten sources. Which one, and why that one?" No credit: "ask the AI again."

Full Standards Text

  • CCSS ELA RI.6.8 — Trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text, distinguishing claims that are supported by reasons and evidence from claims that are not. Taught: Fact-desk method steps 1 and 4 (underline claims; test the ending). Assessed: the RUN/FIX/KILL stamp + note on each draft ("does the ending still stand?").
  • ISTE 1.3.b — Students evaluate the accuracy, perspective, credibility and relevance of information, media, data or other resources. Taught: lateral reading, step 3. Assessed: the "Second source" column on each draft (key above) + Exit Ticket #2.
  • ISTE 1.3.d — Students build knowledge by actively exploring real-world issues and problems, developing ideas and theories and pursuing answers and solutions. Taught: The Fact Desk activity — desks pursue verification and defend verdicts in "Verdicts out loud." Assessed: Exit Ticket #2 (a real claim from their own week and where they'd check it).
