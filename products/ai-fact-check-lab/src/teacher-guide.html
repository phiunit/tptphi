Why This Lesson

Every AI job runs human → AI → human: a person starts it (the prompt) and a person ends it (the check). This lesson trains the second human job. The core insight students leave with: AI predicts likely words, not verified words — so smoothness is zero evidence of truth. Lateral reading (leave the answer, confirm against a second, independent source) is the same move professional fact-checkers make, and it works on AI answers, group-chat rumors, and feeds alike.

Running the Fact Desk

Play the managing editor, not the answer key. When a desk asks "is this one true?", answer "that's a checkable claim — where's your second source?" Keep a deadline energy: "Presses roll in five!"

Watch for the two failure modes. The trusting desk clears everything ("it sounded right") — send them back to step 1: did you underline the dates and names? The paranoid desk kills everything ("it's all sus") — that's what RANK is for: you can't check every claim before deadline, so you check the ones that would hurt most. The goal is calibrated skepticism, not panic.

The fabricated quote in Draft #3 is the boss level. Many students treat quotation marks as proof. Rule for the desk: a quote is a claim — no traceable source, no print.

Answer Key — Warm-Up Paragraph (read-aloud)

Planted errorThe true fact
"The closest planet to the Sun is Venus, which is also the hottest."Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun. (Venus really is the hottest — its thick atmosphere traps heat. The half-truth is what makes this one slippery.)
"Saturn… is the only planet in our solar system that has [rings]."Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune also have rings — faint ones. Saturn's are just the most visible.

True claims a desk should mark SAFE or confirm: eight planets; Mars is red from rusty iron dust on its surface; sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach Earth.

Answer Key — The Three Drafts (every planted error)

DraftPlanted errorThe true fact
#1 Priya"Astronauts first landed on the Moon in 1959"The first crewed Moon landing was Apollo 11 in 1969.
#1 Priya"the Moon… produces its own light"The Moon makes no light of its own — it reflects sunlight.
#2 Theo"Mount Everest… rises in the Andes range in South America"Everest is in the Himalayas, in Asia (on the Nepal–China border). The Andes are in South America.
#2 Theo"Australia's capital city is Sydney"Australia's capital is Canberra. Sydney is its largest city — a classic mix-up.
#3 Marcus"Thomas Edison invented the telephone in 1876"Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone in 1876. Edison is known for the phonograph and practical electric lighting.
#3 MarcusQuote: "The telephone is the finest of all my inventions."Fabricated — we invented this quote for the exercise; it has no source. AI does the same thing: it can generate real-sounding quotes nobody said. No traceable source = no print.

True claims, safe to confirm: Draft #1 — the Moon is Earth's only natural satellite; Neil Armstrong walked first; twelve people have walked on the Moon. Draft #2 — Pacific is the largest ocean; the Nile flows north through Egypt and is one of the world's longest rivers; seven continents; Asia is the largest. Draft #3 — Edison invented the phonograph in 1877, the first machine to both record and play back sound; the Wright brothers flew in 1903 near Kitty Hawk, North Carolina.

Verdict Guidance

Expected verdicts: Draft #1 — FIX (topic holds; correct the date and the light claim). Draft #2 — FIX (correct Everest's range and the capital). Draft #3 — KILL (the headline claim is false and its supporting quote is fabricated; there's no story left to fix). No draft earns RUN today — tell the class that's realistic. Accept a well-argued FIX on #3 ("rewrite it about Bell"), then push: if the lead claim and the quote both fail, is that a fix or a brand-new story?

Score the evidence, not just the stamp: proficient desks name the specific claim, the correction, and a second source; developing desks stamp verdicts on vibes.

Full Standards Text

  • CCSS ELA RI.6.8 — Trace and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text, distinguishing claims that are supported by reasons and evidence from claims that are not. Taught: fact-desk method steps 1–2, modeled on the warm-up paragraph. Assessed: underlined claims, C/S marks, and verdict notes on all three drafts.
  • ISTE 1.3.b — Students evaluate the accuracy, perspective, credibility and relevance of information, media, data or other resources. Taught: lateral reading, step 3 ("don't ask the suspect"). Assessed: "Second source" columns on each draft + Exit Ticket #2.
  • ISTE 1.3.d — Students build knowledge by actively exploring real-world issues and problems, developing ideas and theories and pursuing answers and solutions. Taught: The Fact Desk activity — desks pursue verification and defend verdicts in the stand-up. Assessed: Exit Ticket #2 (a real claim from their own week) + verdict stand-up evidence.
