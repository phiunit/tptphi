Hallucination Hunters

A 45-minute AI fact-checking lesson. The hook: students get hired at a newsroom fact desk, and nothing runs until they clear it. They learn why AI can be confidently wrong — and the four-step method that catches it.

At a Glance

Objectives

  • Students can explain why AI "hallucinates": it predicts likely words, so it can be confidently wrong.
  • Students can identify the checkable claims in a text — names, dates, numbers, places, quotes — and rank them by risk.
  • Students can lateral-read a claim (verify it against a second, independent source) and deliver an evidence-based verdict: RUN, FIX, or KILL.

Warm-Up — The Story That Almost Ran (7 min)

Read this aloud, smooth and confident, like a news anchor. Then ask for a vote: "This is going in tomorrow's paper. Print it?"

"Our solar system has eight planets orbiting one star, the Sun. The closest planet to the Sun is Venus, which is also the hottest. Mars is called the Red Planet because rusty iron dust covers its surface. Saturn is famous for its rings — it is the only planet in our solar system that has them. Sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach Earth."

Most classes vote print. Reveal: two claims are false. Mercury is closest to the Sun (Venus really is the hottest — the half-truth makes it slippery), and Saturn isn't alone: Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune have faint rings too.

Bridge: "That paragraph sounded right because it was SMOOTH, not because it was TRUE. AI talks like that all the time — it predicts likely words, it doesn't check them. Today you're the fact desk: nothing runs until you clear it."

Direct Instruction — Why AI Lies Smoothly + The Fact-Desk Method (10 min)

Why it happens (3 min): "AI is autocomplete with confidence. It predicts the next likely word — the way your phone guesses your next text. When the likely words happen to be true, great. When they're not, it says the wrong thing in the exact same confident voice. That's called a hallucination." (If your class met the trickster genie in Lesson 1: same trickster, new disguise.)

The method (7 min): write the four steps on the board and model steps 1–2 on the warm-up paragraph:

1. UNDERLINE — mark every checkable claim: names, dates, numbers, places, quotes.

2. RANK — you can't check everything before deadline. Mark the claims that would hurt most if wrong CHECK; mark common-knowledge, low-risk ones SAFE.

3. LATERAL-READ — leave the passage. Verify each CHECK claim against a second, independent source: an encyclopedia, a textbook, a new search tab.

4. VERDICT — RUN (clean), FIX (correct the errors, then run), or KILL (the core claim fails — cut the story).

Say it plainly: "Don't ask the suspect if he's innocent. Asking the same AI 'are you sure?' is not a check. Lateral reading means you leave the answer and open a second, independent source. That's the move real fact-checkers make."

Activity — The Fact Desk (20 min)

Pairs become fact-check desks at The Comet, the school paper. Editor-in-chief Deja's memo is on the worksheet: three writers used an AI helper on their drafts, and nothing runs until the desk clears it.

  1. Desk shift 1 (7 min): Both partners work Draft #1 (Priya's Moon column) together — underline, mark C or S, log where they'd verify, stamp a verdict with evidence.
  2. Desk shift 2 (9 min): Split the load: one partner leads Draft #2 (Theo's geography quiz), the other Draft #3 (Marcus's invention feature). Then swap and double-check each other before stamping.
  3. Verdict stand-up (4 min): Each desk announces one verdict plus its single best piece of evidence. Tally RUN / FIX / KILL on the board and argue out any disagreements.

Debrief (4 min)

  • Which planted error was hardest to catch — and what made it slippery?
  • Where else does confident-but-wrong show up? Feeds, group chats, AI homework helpers…
  • What's your personal "before I repeat it" rule from now on?

Exit Ticket (4 min)

Bottom of worksheet: (1) in their own words, why can AI sound 100% sure and still be wrong; (2) one claim they've seen online this week and the exact lateral-reading move they'd make on it.

Differentiation

  • Support: Assign Drafts #1–2 only, and share the hint from the answer key: "each draft hides exactly two problems."
  • Extension: Write your own smooth four-sentence passage with ONE planted error; trade desks and hunt.
  • ELL: Claim types are concrete categories (names, dates, numbers, places, quotes) — have students label the TYPE above each underline; partners read passages aloud.
  • With devices: Desks lateral-read their top CHECK claims live during shifts. Without: they log where they'd verify — the method is the lesson.
