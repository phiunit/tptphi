What's in the Crate — One-Genre DJ improv tips, Crate Check answer key, real-world debrief guide, and exit ticket exemplars.

Why This Lesson

Students usually hear "AI is biased" as "somebody made it mean." This lesson swaps the villain for a mechanism: input → patterns → output. Once a student has personally failed Tomás because their crate held no Spanish tracks, "biased training data" stops being vocabulary and becomes something they did — and know how to fix. The transfer question works on every AI tool they'll ever meet: what's in the crate?

Playing the One-Genre DJ

Three moves carry the bit: serve with total confidence (the DJ has no idea it's failing — never apologize), announce the track like it's exactly right ("Grandma's birthday? VOLUME WAR."), and upgrade nothing — a slow-song request gets the same tempo, cheerfully. Keep it to 3–4 requests so the joke lands and moves. The gold moment is when a student says the crate is the problem, not the DJ: hand them the imaginary aux cord and applaud — they've just said the thesis of the lesson out loud.

Scoring the Gap Report & Doctor the Crate

  • Proficient: names WHO is served/left out (people, not just song types), and the 3 new cards target different gaps (tempo, language, style — not three slow songs).
  • Developing: names missing song types but not the people behind the requests; new cards fix one gap three times.
  • Beginning: "the crate needs more songs" with no who or what.

Exemplars & Sentence Frames

Say back + build frames (support / ELL): "I heard you say ____." · "Building on that, ____." · "This crate serves ____ but not ____." For students overwhelmed by 12 cards, pre-circle Cool Down (Kinda) and the three Gym tracks as landmarks before the game.

Doctor the Crate exemplar cards: "Luna Lullaby — acoustic ballad · slow · study" / "Cumbia del Cumpleaños — cumbia · medium · family party, Spanish lyrics" / "Porch Strings — folk · quiet · sing-along." Three cards, three different gaps.

Exit Ticket 1 exemplar: "INPUT → PATTERNS → OUTPUT. The AI studies its crate of examples, learns what usually goes with what, and answers by predicting what matches those patterns. It doesn't understand anything — it predicts."

Exit Ticket 2 exemplar: "Whoever filled its crate used football examples, so football patterns are all it can serve. Students who don't follow football — or whose interests never made it into the crate — get worse help than everyone else."

The Real Crates — Debrief Guide

Voice assistants: they understand best the voices and accents that filled their training audio. Less-common accents, kids' voices, and people with speech differences get misheard more often — an accessibility gap that traces straight back to the crate.

Photo tools: face-detection features have historically worked worse on darker skin tones when the training photos leaned lighter — and they improved when companies rebuilt their datasets to represent everyone. The fix for a crate problem is fixing the crate.

Your feed: a recommendation feed's crate is your watch history. A one-genre history makes a one-genre feed — same mechanism, running in students' pockets right now.

"Did someone do this on purpose?" Usually not. Teams collected the data that was easiest to get, and easy-to-get data leans toward whoever is already over-represented. No villain required — which is exactly why real AI teams now audit their datasets before shipping. (Checking the crate is a real job.)

"Isn't AI creative, though?" It can blend crate patterns into combinations nobody put in — like a mashup of two songs. But mashup ingredients still come from the crate; it can't mash up a genre it has never heard.

Handle with care: some students in the room are the people these gaps mishear or mislabel. Keep the frame "the crate failed them, and crates can be fixed" — never "the tech doesn't like you."
