Robots Do the Boring Work

A 45-minute AI-and-future-jobs lesson. The hook: your class runs Big Dream Studio, a small recording studio with too much to do. They decide which tasks get handed to AI, which get AI's help, and which stay fully human — and they own the tradeoff behind every call.

At a Glance

TimeMaterialsTech neededPrep
45 minStudio Task Board worksheet (1 per student)None — fully unplugged by designPrint worksheets. That's it.

Objectives

  • Students can sort real job tasks into automate, augment, and keep human — and explain the difference.
  • Students can compare the tradeoffs of an automation choice: who wins, who loses, what could go wrong.
  • Students can defend a sorting decision aloud with a clear claim and specific supporting details.

Warm-Up — The Most Boring Job Ever (7 min)

Ask: "What's the most boring job you can think of?" Take 4–5 answers and write them on the board. Then pick two or three and ask the follow-up:

"Is it boring because it REPEATS — same thing, over and over, no decisions? Or is it 'boring' but secretly full of people stuff — helping, noticing, talking someone down?"

Sort them as a class: repeats → robot bait. People stuff → hold on to it. (Data entry repeats. A toll collector repeats — but also waves at every regular. Let students argue; the arguing is the point.)

Bridge: "Here's the honest version, no robot apocalypse: AI changes jobs more than it deletes them. The boring, repeating parts get automated. The human parts — teaching, taste, live connection — become the job. But every time a business automates something, somebody wins, somebody loses, and something could go wrong. Today YOU make those calls, for a real business: a recording studio."

Direct Instruction — Three Columns and the Tradeoff Lens (10 min)

Draw three columns on the board and define them:

AUTOMATE — boring and repeating. Same task, over and over, no judgment calls. Hand it to AI.

AUGMENT — the human decides, AI assists. AI drafts or suggests; a person with taste makes the final call.

KEEP HUMAN — mentorship, taste, live connection. The human IS the product.

Then model one studio task through the tradeoff lens. Task: "Text this week's clients their session reminders." Column: AUTOMATE — but don't stop at the column:

Who wins?Who loses something?What could go wrong?
Jaylen at the front desk — gets hours back every week for actual clients.The new intern — sending those texts was how she learned every client's name.A cheerful auto-reminder goes to a client whose session was canceled. Awkward.
Say it plainly: "The column is the easy part. The tradeoff is the real thinking. In this room, 'because robots' is not a reason. 'Who wins, who loses, what could go wrong' — that's a reason."

Main Activity — The Studio Task Board (15 min)

  1. Sort (8 min): Pairs meet the Big Dream Studio crew — Nia (owner), Ravi (engineer), Jaylen (front desk) — and sort tasks A–L into the three columns. House rule: every column gets at least two tasks. Partners who disagree must talk it out; either can be right.
  2. Tradeoff cards (7 min): Each pair picks TWO of their own calls and writes the tradeoff: who wins, who loses, what could go wrong (worksheet p.2).

Share-Out — Defend the Board (8 min)

Pairs defend ONE choice aloud, about 45 seconds each, using the claim frame from the worksheet: "Task ___ belongs in ___ because ___ — and the tradeoff is ___." Get through 5–7 pairs; coach eye contact and volume, and score speakers with the Defense Rubric (Teacher Guide p.2). Pairs who don't present hand in their written claim frame instead. Gallery-walk variant: post the boards, tour for 3 minutes, then the 3 most-argued-about calls get defended.

Exit Ticket (5 min)

Bottom of worksheet p.2: students run the three columns on a job someone in their life actually has — one line per column, plus a why for the human one.

Differentiation

  • Support: Sort 8 tasks instead of 12 (skip B, F, J, L); the claim frame and column definitions are printed on the worksheet.
  • Extension: Find one task where automating it saves money now but hurts the studio in a year — explain the chain.
  • ELL: Sorting letters keeps the writing load low; the claim frame doubles as a language frame; pairs may argue in their strongest language and present in English.
  • No-device classrooms: Built in — the entire lesson runs on paper and talk.
