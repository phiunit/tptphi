Teach AI Your Taste — exemplar taste-file entries, coaching the "AI editor" role, the copy-ready AI feedback prompt, scoring, and full standards text.

Why This Lesson

When AI can generate anything, the skill that still separates students is taste — knowing what's them and saying it precisely. The Taste File makes a student's style visible, and the editor loop (draft yourself → seek specific feedback → revise yourself) is W.6.5 in miniature, with the classroom AI policy built in: AI is your editor, not your ghostwriter.

Coaching the "AI Editor" Role

The editor has three rules, and holding them IS the rigor: use only the taste file (a note that can't point at a section gets cut), exactly 2 notes (editors prioritize), and notes, not rewrites (the second the editor writes a sentence, they've become a ghostwriter).

Watch for the classic slip — partners rewriting. Redirect: "You're the editor. Hand the pen back."

Exemplar Taste-File Entries

There is no single right taste — score for specificity. This is the target level:

StudentVibe + pet peeve"So me" sentence
Jaylenfunny, blunt, lowkey. "Never use words I'd have to look up to sound smart.""Saturday I made the world's saddest pancake and ate it anyway."
Priyadramatic, poetic, big idea first. "Don't shorten my sentences — they're long on purpose.""The weekend arrived like a plot twist nobody asked for, and I loved every second."
Tomáshype, short + punchy, lists. "No baby-talk. I'm 12, not 5.""Game. Winner. Rematch. That was the whole weekend."

Scoring Guidance

  • Proficient: taste file specific enough for a stranger to imitate; the starred revision visibly moves the draft toward the file.
  • Developing: taste file real but vague ("funny, good"); notes generic; revision changes words, not voice.
  • Beginning: taste file restates the word bank; the revision is the editor's sentence, not the writer's.

With Devices — The Copy-Ready AI Feedback Prompt

For Activity 2, Step 2 with devices — students fill the brackets from their worksheet:

"Here is my taste file: [your 5 sections]. Here is my draft: [your 3–4 sentences]. You are my editor, not my ghostwriter. Give exactly 2 specific notes on where the draft does not match my taste file, naming the section each note points at. Do not rewrite anything."

What a Good Note Looks Like

Ghost-note (reject)Editor's note (accept)
"Make it funnier. Say: 'Welcome to my chaotic channel!'""Section 3 says no textbook voice — 'I enjoy various hobbies' is textbook voice. Which hobby, said your way?"

Full Standards Text

  • CCSS ELA W.6.5 — With some guidance and support from peers and adults, develop and strengthen writing as needed by planning, revising, editing, rewriting, or trying a new approach. (Editing for conventions should demonstrate command of Language standards 1-3 up to and including grade 6 here.) Taught: Direct Instruction beat 3 + Activity 2. Assessed: Step 3 starred revision + Exit Ticket.
  • ISTE 1.6.b — Students create original works or responsibly repurpose or remix digital resources into new creations. Taught: Activity 1, Build Your Taste File + Activity 2, Step 1 draft. Assessed: Editor's Loop Checklist rows 1 and 3 (own draft, own revision).
  • CCSS ELA SL.6.1 — Engage effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grade 6 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly. Taught: Editor's Chair partner-note protocol. Assessed: the two written notes + the marked revision.

Support — Editor Frames & Tone Words

Extra tone words: honest · goofy · calm · fierce · curious · sharp. Note frame: "Your taste file says ____ (section #), but ____ reads ____. Try ____." Revision frame: "I changed ____ to ____ because my taste file says ____."
