Teach AI Your Taste

A 45-minute revision and feedback lesson. The hook: your For-You Page didn't guess your taste — you taught it, one like at a time. Students write their taste down in a one-page Taste File, then run the loop that makes AI safe for schoolwork: draft it yourself, get specific feedback, revise it yourself.

Objectives

  • Students can explain why vague input gets vague feedback — from an app, an AI, or a person.
  • Students can build a Taste File: a one-page style guide an editor can actually use.
  • Students can run the editor loop — draft, seek specific feedback, revise — with the human doing the writing at both ends.

Warm-Up — The Algorithm Knows You (6 min)

Open with: "Raise your hand if your feed knows you a little too well." Take 2–3 answers to these:

"Two people download the same app on the same day. A month later, one feed is all horror edits and the other is all cooking videos. The app didn't guess. How did it learn?"

Land on: every like, skip, rewatch, and follow is a signal. You taught it your taste — and the more precisely you signal, the better the feed serves you. DJs do the same thing on purpose: the crate a DJ builds IS their taste, written down in songs.

Bridge: "Here's the flip side: AI has never watched your feed. It knows nothing about your taste until you teach it. Today you write your taste down on one page — and then you use it to make YOUR OWN writing better."

Direct Instruction — Generic In, Generic Out (9 min)

1 — Generic in, generic out. Ask an AI "give me feedback on my paragraph" and you get fortune-cookie notes: "add detail," "vary your sentences." True for anyone, useful to no one.

2 — The Taste File. One page: your tone, formats, pet peeves, one "so me" sentence. Your DJ crate for words — hand it to any editor and the notes finally fit YOU.

3 — The editor loop. YOU draft → editor gives notes → YOU revise. Say it plainly: "AI is your editor, not your ghostwriter."

Model the difference on the board: "Make it more interesting" (generic) vs. "Your taste file says funny and blunt — sentence 2 sounds like a homework robot. Say it like you'd text it" (taste-file note).

Activity 1 — Build Your Taste File (12 min)

  1. Solo on worksheet page 1: circle tone words, pick formats, write two pet peeves, then So Me / Not Me — one sentence in their real voice, the same sentence in a voice that isn't.
  2. Circulate and push for specifics: "lowkey" beats "good"; "don't make me sound like a textbook" beats "be nice."
  3. Minute 10: two volunteers read a So Me sentence — the class hears how different real taste sounds.

Activity 2 — The Editor's Chair (14 min)

  1. Draft (5 min): Solo, worksheet page 2 — 3–4 sentences: an About Me for a channel or profile page they'd actually make. No AI, no partner.
  2. Editor's notes (4 min): Pairs swap drafts AND taste files. The partner plays the AI editor: exactly 2 notes, each pointing at a numbered taste-file section, no rewriting. With devices: students give a real AI their taste file and draft using the copy-ready prompt (Teacher Guide, p.2) and it returns 2 notes.
  3. Revise (5 min): Students rewrite their own draft and star (*) every change. The editor never touches the draft.

Exit Ticket (4 min)

Bottom of worksheet page 2: students name one revision they made and explain why it fits their taste file.

Differentiation

  • Support: Extra tone words and editor sentence frames (Teacher Guide, p.2).
  • Extension: Write the anti-taste-file — three notes for the voice you never want.
  • ELL: Tone words double as vocabulary support; taste files may include home-language words.
  • No-device classrooms: Built in — the partner IS the AI, and the taste file is all the "training data" they get.
