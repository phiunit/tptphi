Teach AI Your Taste

Your For-You Page didn't guess your taste — you taught it, one like at a time. Unless you tell it (or it has saved notes about you), an AI doesn't know yours. Students write theirs down in a one-page Taste File, then draft, get two notes, and revise. 45 min; stands alone.

Objectives — students can

  • Explain why vague input gets vague feedback — from an app, an AI, or a person. (Last Check #3)
  • Build a Taste File: a one-page style guide an editor can actually use. (Worksheet p.1, Sections 1–5)
  • Run the editor loop — draft, two specific notes, revise — the human writing at both ends. (Steps 1–3, Last Check #1–2)

Warm-Up — The Algorithm Knows You (6 min)

Open: "Raise your hand if your feed knows you a little too well." Then: "Two people download the same app on the same day. A month later one feed is all horror edits, the other all cooking videos. The app didn't guess — how did it learn?" Take 2–3 answers. Land on: every like, skip, rewatch and follow is a signal. You taught it your taste.

Bridge: "Flip side: unless you tell it — or it has saved notes about you — an AI doesn't know your taste. Today you write yours down on one page, then use it to make YOUR OWN writing better."

What if "my feed is random"? "Random is a signal too — what do you skip in the first second?" Nobody raises a hand? Name your own feed first.

Transition: "So what happens when the editor knows nothing about you? Watch."

Direct Instruction — Generic In, Generic Out (8 min)

1 — Generic in, generic out. "Give me feedback on my paragraph" gets notes for a generic writer: "add detail." True for anyone, useful to no one.

2 — The Taste File. One page: tone, formats, pet peeves, one "so me" sentence. Hand it to any editor and the notes finally fit YOU. (If your class did the genie lesson: it's your Requirements, written once.)

3 — The editor loop. YOU draft → editor gives notes → YOU revise. Say it plainly: "AI is your editor, not your ghostwriter."

Watch one get fixed — on the board: "Make it more interesting" vs. "Your taste file says funny and blunt — sentence 2 sounds like a homework robot. Say it like you'd text it." Ask which note they could act on.

What if "the AI I use already knows me"? "Some tools save notes about you. A fresh chat usually doesn't. Either way, the file makes sure the notes fit."

Transition: "Page 1. Your taste, written down — five sections, twelve minutes, solo. Nobody swaps yet."

Activity 1 — Build Your Taste File (12 min)

  1. Solo, worksheet page 1, in order: S1 tick 3 tone words + one of their own · S2 tick formats · S3 two pet peeves · S4 So Me / Not Me (their weekend in their voice, then as a robot, a textbook or a fancy poet) · S5 what they're into right now.
  2. Circulate and push for specifics: "quiet" beats "good"; "don't make me sound like a textbook" beats "be nice."
  3. Minute 10: two volunteers read a So Me sentence — the class hears how different real taste sounds.

What if a student is stuck on So Me? "Read me the last text you sent a friend. That's your voice." Finished early? Extension below.

Transition: "Flip to page 2. Three to four sentences: the About Me for a channel or profile you'd really make. Match your taste file. Solo — five minutes."

Activity 2 — The Editor's Chair (15 min)

  1. Draft (5 min): Solo, page 2, Step 1. No AI, no partner.
    What if "I don't have a channel"? "The one you WOULD make — a game name, a team, a club, a pet's account."
    Transition: "Pens down. Swap ALL your pages with your partner. You're their editor now — and page 1 is the only thing you're allowed to know about them."
  2. Editor's notes (5 min): Editors read the partner's taste file, then write exactly 2 notes on the partner's page 2, each naming a section, no rewriting (3 min). Then the 1-minute talk, scripted on the sheet: editor reads both notes aloud; writer asks ONE question ("Which sentence?" / "What would fit?"); editor answers from the taste file only. With devices: run Step 2 once on YOUR projected account — one volunteer's taste file and draft in the copy-ready prompt (Teacher Guide); the class checks that each note names a section. Students never need an account.
    What if the editor starts rewriting? "You're the editor. Hand the pen back." No section number? "Which section? No section, no note."
    Transition (at 3 min): "Editors, pens down. Read your two notes out loud. Writers, one question." Then: "Swap back. Page 3 — your draft, your pen. Star every change."
  3. Revise (5 min): Students rewrite their own draft on page 3, star (*) every change, then tick all four checklist rows. The editor never touches it.
    What if a student changes nothing? "Your editor pointed at section 3. Read it out loud. Now change one sentence to match it."
    Transition: "Four ticks. Then the Last Check at the bottom: three answers, your own words, four minutes."

Last Check — Exit Ticket (4 min)

Bottom of page 3, solo: #1 one change they made (old words → new words); #2 why it fits their taste file, naming the section; #3 why the note "make it better" would NOT have helped them (Objective 1). Collect; key in the Teacher Guide.

What if a student writes "I made it better"? "Which word changed? Write the old word and the new one."

Differentiation

  • Support: Extra tone words, a note frame and a revision frame (Teacher Guide). One pet peeve and one note is fine.
  • Extension: The anti-taste-file — three notes for the voice you never want to sound like.
  • ELL: Tone words double as vocabulary; home-language words welcome in the taste file; the talk's two question frames are printed on the sheet.
  • No-device classrooms: Built in — the partner IS the AI, and the taste file is the only thing the editor is allowed to know about them. Skip the "with devices" line; nothing else changes.
