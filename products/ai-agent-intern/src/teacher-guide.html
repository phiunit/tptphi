Why This Lesson

It stands alone — every skill it uses is re-taught in miniature on the page. The idea: an AI agent does a multi-step job on its own — and where your card is vague, it guesses forward. So the human job moves from doing the work to leading it: write the job down (the quest card), split it into roles, and check what comes back before it ships. For the adults in the room, a quest card is what work calls a brief or a spec, and this loop is delegation — a skill students may one day use to run real projects and real AI tools; nobody can promise exactly how those tools will look. "Party leader" is simply the version of that job every 12-year-old already understands. (If your class did the earlier lessons, they will notice the rerun: the quest card is the genie-proof prompt, the accuracy check is the fact desk, the taste check is the feedback eye.)

Exemplar Quest Card (Game Night — the board model)

GOAL: get 6th graders to actually show up to Game Night. WHAT WE KNOW: Friday, gym, 6–8 pm; we have board games and two TVs. RULES OF THE RUN: school-appropriate, big readable title, 40 words max. DONE LOOKS LIKE: one flyer draft, ready to photocopy.

How to score any quest card (Step 2 and Exit #1): hand it to the most literal reader in the room. If they can twist it, or have to guess, a part is missing or vague. Proficient = all four parts, specific enough that a stranger could do it tomorrow. Developing = parts present but vague ("make it good," "soon"). Beginning = a wish, not a quest card ("help me with math").

CheckEvidenceVerdict
ACCURACY"Steps 1–4 use Friday, gym, 6–8 pm straight off the card."SHIP IT
TASTE"Step 3 says 'write the pitch' but never says to 6th graders — it could be for anybody."RUN IT BACK
DONE-NESS"All four card parts are covered, and the NOT ON THE CARD flag is a plus."SHIP IT

KEEP: "The time guesses add up to the 23 minutes we have, exactly like the card asked." FIX: "Step 3 needs the words 'for 6th graders' — the card names the audience and the plan dropped it." Score: evidence is quoted or pointed to, one verdict per check, and the FIX names the exact change plus why. A note that says "good job, be more specific" is a RUN IT BACK on the leader.

Answer Key — Exit Ticket (worksheet p.2)

ItemWhat a good answer looks like
#1 — quest card for a real task (Objective 1)Exemplar (a study plan): GOAL: pass Friday's fractions quiz with at least a B. WHAT WE KNOW: 7th grade math; I have the review sheet and 30 minutes a night, Monday to Thursday. RULES OF THE RUN: only fractions with different denominators, no calculator, nothing due before Wednesday. DONE LOOKS LIKE: a four-night plan, 5 practice problems a night, answers on a separate line. Score with the quest-card scale on p.1 (could a stranger do it tomorrow from the card alone?).
#2 — which role first, and why (Objective 2)Any role earns credit if the reason fits the task: scout first for a fact-heavy flyer ("I need the real dates before anyone plans"); planner first for a schedule ("the steps ARE the job"); critic first when the audience is picky ("my little brother won't follow a boring chore list"). The reason is what scores; a role with no reason is Developing.
#3 — the check it would most likely fail (Objective 3)Any check with something real to look for: "ACCURACY — the quiz date and which chapter"; "TASTE — is it written for a 12-year-old or for a teacher?"; "DONE-NESS — did it give all four nights, or stop at two?" Proficient names the check AND the specific thing to look for; a check alone is Developing.

When It Goes Sideways

The class doesn't divide by 3 → a team of 4 runs two critics (both read the plan; they'll disagree — great). A team of 2 drops the critic; the leader does the taste check personally.

A leader turns harsh → the note requires KEEP before FIX, and the leader reads it aloud to their party member. Feedback you say to a face stays fair.

A team finishes early → rewrite the quest card so tight the critic can't land a note — or design a fourth squad role and its one-line job.

A card is hopelessly vague and the squad stalls → don't rescue it. "It's not on the card" is the only answer to questions mid-round; in Leader review the RUN IT BACK lands on the card, not the party member. That public, fixable failure is the lesson. (With devices: the projected AI invents a fact — best case. "Which missing rule let it do that?" Add the rule, run it again.)

Full Standards Text

  • ISTE 1.5.c — Students break problems into component parts, extract key information, and develop descriptive models to understand complex systems or facilitate problem-solving. Taught: the Party Leader's Loop instruction (a project broken into four card parts and three roles). Assessed: the Step 2 quest card and Exit Ticket #1, scored with the quest-card scale on p.1.
  • ISTE 1.7.c — Students contribute constructively to project teams, assuming various roles and responsibilities to work effectively toward a common goal. Taught: Run the Quest (every student works one role, then leads one review). Assessed: the Step 3 role output (scored against the exemplar squad output, p.1), the Step 4 CHECK rubric and run-it-back note, and Exit Ticket #2.
  • CCSS ELA W.6.4 — Produce clear and coherent writing in which the development, organization, and style are appropriate to task, purpose, and audience. (Grade-specific expectations for writing types are defined in standards 1-3 above.) Taught: quest-card modeling in direct instruction (goal, audience, rules, output). Assessed: Exit Ticket #1 — a four-part quest card written for a real task and audience, scored Proficient / Developing / Beginning.

Support & ELL: Quest Card Sentence Starters

GOAL: "When this quest is done, ____ will happen."  ·  WHAT WE KNOW: "This is for ____. The team already has ____."

RULES OF THE RUN: "It must ____. It must NOT ____. Due ____."  ·  DONE LOOKS LIKE: "Hand back a ____ (flyer / list / plan / paragraph)."

ELL students may draft the card in their strongest language first, then translate — the four labels double as language frames.
