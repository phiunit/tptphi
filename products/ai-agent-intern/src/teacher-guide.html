Why This Lesson

This is the unit's final boss, and it's deliberately a rerun. The brief is Lesson 1's P.R.O. framework grown up. The accuracy check is Lesson 3's fact-desk habit. The taste check is Lesson 4's feedback eye. Choosing what to hand the intern is Lesson 5's boring-work sort. Students aren't learning four new skills — they're discovering that the four they already have make them a manager. (Teaching it standalone works fine: every skill is re-taught in miniature on the page.) The stance underneath: everyone should be able to manage AI agents the way managers manage interns — brief, check, feedback — because those are the two jobs AI hasn't changed: a human starts the work, and a human signs off.

Exemplar Brief (Zine Issue #1 mission)

GOAL: a first issue of the class zine that makes other 6th graders want to submit their own work. CONTEXT: 8 photocopied pages; we already have 3 comics and 2 poems from classmates. CONSTRAINTS: school-appropriate, every page credits a student by name, cover must show the zine's title, done by Friday. DELIVERABLE: a page-by-page plan plus a rough cover sketch.

How to score any brief: hand it to the most literal reader in the room. If they can twist it, or have to guess, a part is missing or vague. All four parts, specific = proficient.

Coaching the Three Roles

Planner — good output is a numbered step list with time guesses, plus "NOT IN BRIEF" flags where the brief is silent. Those flags are gold: they're next draft's fixes.

Researcher — good output is a fact list where every item is marked ✓ verified (with where) or ? needs checking. If a "fact" has no source, it's a guess wearing a costume — send it back.

Critic — good output is 3 upgrades tied to the audience the brief names ("6th graders won't read 40 words of rules — cut to 15"). Vague praise and "make it better" don't count.

Enforce the silence. When an agent asks a question mid-round, answer: "It's not in the brief." The frustration is the point — vague briefs fail in public, and students feel exactly why bosses write better ones. Don't rescue a weak brief; the boss review makes the failure visible and fixable.

Running the Room (What-Ifs)

Class doesn't divide by 3? A team of 4 runs two critics (they'll disagree — great). A team of 2 drops the critic; the boss does the taste check personally.

A boss turns harsh? The send-back note requires KEEP before FIX, and the boss reads the note aloud to their agent. Feedback you say to a face stays professional.

A team finishes early? Extension: rewrite the brief so tight the critic can't land a single note — or design a fourth agent role and write its card.

Exemplar Send-Back Note (share after round 1)

KEEP: "The schedule works and the title is big and readable, exactly like the brief asked." FIX: "The flyer says 'games all night' — Game Night ends at 8 pm. Fix the time, then re-check every fact against the brief." A specific fact, a specific fix — the agent never has to guess. That's the whole art.

Scoring the Exit Ticket

  • Proficient: all four parts labeled and specific enough that a stranger could do the task tomorrow.
  • Developing: parts present but vague ("make it good," "soon," no deliverable named).
  • Beginning: a wish, not a brief ("help me with math").

Full Standards Text

  • ISTE 1.5.c — Students break problems into component parts and develop descriptions of solutions. Taught: the Manager's Loop direct instruction (brief parts + role split). Assessed: Brief Builder, worksheet p.1 (all four parts, decomposed into three role cards).
  • ISTE 1.7.c — Students contribute constructively to project teams, assuming various roles and responsibilities to work effectively toward a common goal. Taught: Run the Project team activity (every student works an agent role AND serves as boss). Assessed: role-card output on paper + completed CHECK rubric and send-back note, worksheet p.2.
  • CCSS ELA W.6.4 — Produce clear and coherent writing in which the development, organization, and style are appropriate to task, purpose, and audience. Taught: brief modeling in direct instruction (goal and context name the task, purpose, audience). Assessed: Exit Ticket brief, worksheet p.2.

Support: Brief Sentence Starters

GOAL: "When this is done, we will have ____ so that ____."

CONTEXT: "This is for ____. They already have / know ____."

CONSTRAINTS: "It must ____. It must NOT ____. Keep it ____ (length/tone). Due ____."

DELIVERABLE: "Hand back a ____ (flyer / list / plan / paragraph), on ____ (paper / a card)."
