Manage AI Like an Intern

A 45-minute delegation capstone. The hook: an AI agent is a capable, tireless, extremely literal intern. Bosses don't do the work twice — they write a clear brief, split the roles, and check the work before it ships. Stands alone; no earlier lesson required.

At a Glance

Objectives

  • Students can write a complete brief — goal, context, constraints, deliverable — that a literal-minded agent could follow without guessing.
  • Students can split a project into agent roles (planner, researcher, critic) and execute a role from the brief alone.
  • Students can review work against a CHECK rubric (accuracy, taste, done-ness) and write a specific send-back note.

Warm-Up — The Intern Did EXACTLY What You Said (7 min)

Tell the class about your new intern: brilliant, tireless, never complains — and painfully literal. Read out three jobs you gave the intern last week, and what came back:

"Take care of the emails in my inbox." → Inbox empty by Monday. Every email deleted. Taken care of.

"Make the poster bigger." → Printed at 400%. It no longer fits through the door.

"Add everyone to the group chat." → Everyone. All 850 students. And the principal.

Then ask: whose fault was that — the intern's, or the instructions? (Classes that met the literal genie in Lesson 1 will call it instantly: the intern IS the genie — it just got a job. New classes need no backstory; the horror stories carry it.)

Bridge: "An AI agent is this intern: fast, tireless, never annoyed — and it does exactly what you SAY. You will never out-work it. You CAN out-manage it. Today you learn the manager's loop: brief, roles, check — then ship it or send it back."

Direct Instruction — The Manager's Loop (10 min)

Write the loop on the board: BRIEF → ROLES → CHECK → SHIP or SEND BACK. Then unpack each stop:

BRIEF. The four things an intern needs in writing: goal (what done looks like), context (who it's for, what already exists), constraints (limits — length, tone, rules, deadline), deliverable (the exact thing handed back). Prompt-writers, this is P.R.O. grown up: purpose split into goal + context, requirements became constraints, output became deliverable.

ROLES. Bosses don't hand one giant job to one intern. Split it: a planner, a researcher, a critic — same brief, one slice each.

CHECK. Before anything ships, the boss checks three things: accuracy (is every stated fact verified?), taste (does it fit the audience and the vibe?), done-ness (is every deliverable actually there?).

SHIP or SEND BACK. Good work ships. Weak work gets a send-back note — what to keep, what to fix, specific enough that the intern never guesses.

Model one brief live on the board:

Cursed instructionManager's brief
"Plan the game night.""GOAL: a one-page flyer that gets 6th graders to show up to Game Night. CONTEXT: Friday, in the gym, 6–8 pm; we already have board games and two TVs. CONSTRAINTS: school-appropriate, big readable title, 40 words max. DELIVERABLE: one flyer draft on paper, ready to photocopy."
Say it plainly: "Bosses don't do the work twice. If you have to redo the intern's work, your brief failed. Fix the brief, not just the work."

Activity — Run the Project (22 min)

  1. Write the brief (6 min): Teams of 3 pick a mission on the worksheet — the Game Night page or class zine issue #1 — and write ONE team brief with all four parts.
  2. Agents work (8 min): Each teammate takes one role card — planner, researcher, critic — and executes ONLY the brief, silently, on their own paper. No questions allowed: agents can't read minds. (In Sana's team: DeShawn plans, Lucia researches, Sana critiques.)
  3. Boss review (8 min): Rotate bosses — Sana reviews DeShawn's output, DeShawn reviews Lucia's, Lucia reviews Sana's. Each boss runs the CHECK rubric on worksheet page 2, rules SHIP or SEND BACK, and writes one send-back note.

Exit Ticket (6 min)

Worksheet page 2: students write one complete brief for a real task in their own life — labeled goal, context, constraints, deliverable — something they'd actually hand to an AI agent this week.

Differentiation

  • Support: Brief sentence starters (Teacher Guide, p.2).
  • Extension: Write a brief so tight the critic can't land a single taste note — or invent a fourth agent role and write its card.
  • ELL: The four brief labels double as language frames; students may draft the brief in their strongest language first, then translate.
  • No-device classrooms: Built in — the teammates ARE the agents.
  • With devices: After the boss review, run the team's brief through a real AI tool and boss-review ITS output with the same CHECK rubric. The loop doesn't change.
