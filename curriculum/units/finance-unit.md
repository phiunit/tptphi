# Unit: Personal Finance & Risk — "Future Money" (6 lessons, grades 6–8)
Bundle target: bundle-finance-line ($12–15). Every lesson: 45 min, no-prep, unplugged variant,
$4–6 (L1 free). Scope matches Jump$tart / CEE personal-finance topic areas and double-dips into
CCSS Math (6.RP ratios, 6.EE relationships, 7.SP probability):
track it → earn it → count what isn't cash → price it → size the risk → find the gap (capstone).

Every number in every student scenario is a **round hypothetical** and is labeled that way on the
page ("pretend numbers — they're easy on purpose"). This unit never quotes a real interest rate,
salary, or statistic.

## Unit thesis
After six lessons a student can take any pile of money or resources they actually control — a $40
week, a bake-sale float, a skill nobody's paying them for yet — and run it through a decision
instead of a feeling. They can chart where it drained to, tell the difference between money they
have to show up for and money that keeps arriving after they leave, list four kinds of wealth they
own that aren't cash and trade one for another, compute what it costs to make one more of something
and price it, name the most they'd risk before they risk it, and then find a real gap between what
something costs and what it's worth and pitch the move with numbers attached. None of that requires
a bank account, a device, or a job — which is exactly why a sixth grader can do it on Monday.

## Unit through-line
**Cost → Value → What You Can Lose.** Every lesson asks the same three questions in the same order:
what did this cost me (money, hours, or a slot in my inventory), what is it worth to somebody else,
and what happens to me if it goes wrong. L1 answers question one, L4 answers question two, L5
answers question three, and L6 makes students answer all three about the same real decision.
Continuity motif: the battery meter from L1 reappears as a small gauge on later worksheets showing
what a choice drained — but each lesson's own analogy carries the concept.

## Lesson 1 — finance-money-battery (FREE lead magnet, $0)
- **Corpus source:** FRAMEWORKS "Second- and third-order effects" (keep asking *and then what?*) and
  "The three-level reflection ladder" as the closing routine; VOICE stance 15 (tie entertainment to
  education) sets the register.
- **Why a teacher buys this:** financial literacy is now a graduation requirement in a growing list
  of states and the teacher who got assigned it has no materials. This is day one, free, printable
  at 7:40 a.m., and it gets kids who "don't do budgets" to fill out a battery meter.
- **Activity:** each student runs a hypothetical $40 week through a battery-drain log (snack, game
  skin, bus, gift, save), shades the meter as each cost lands, then re-runs the identical week after
  changing exactly one choice and compares the two ending percentages.
- **Assessment:** exit ticket — name your biggest drain, name the one swap that saves the most, and
  show the percent it saved.
- **Unplugged:** it is already paper-only; the device variant is optional (phone calculator).

## Lesson 2 — finance-owns-the-ball ($4)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Hours ≠ Dollars"; FRAMEWORKS "Eight kinds of capital, and decoupling
  money from time."
- **Why a teacher buys this:** the earning-income standard usually gets taught as a list of job
  titles. This gives it a graph, a crossover point, and an argument kids will actually run at each
  other — one lesson that covers income *and* a math standard.
- **Activity:** two teams run six rounds of a hypothetical earning sim side by side — Team Shift must
  be present every round to get paid, Team Ball rents out one thing it owns — and both plot income
  per round on the same grid to find the round where the lines cross.
- **Assessment:** the two-line graph plus a written claim: at which round does owning beat showing
  up, and what would have to change to move that round earlier?
- **Unplugged:** dice-and-index-card sim, no devices at any point.

## Lesson 3 — finance-inventory-check ($5)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Eight Kinds of Capital"; FRAMEWORKS "Eight kinds of capital"; VOICE
  stance 29 (creative confidence spilling into every other part of life).
- **Why a teacher buys this:** it is the money lesson for the class where money is a sore subject.
  Every kid, including the one with an empty wallet, finishes with a full inventory sheet — and it
  doubles as an advisory/SEL day that still cites a finance standard.
- **Activity:** students fill five inventory slots (things I can do, people who'd vouch for me, tools
  I can get to, things I know that most kids don't, what I'm trusted with), then design one three-link
  trade that turns one slot into another and label what each side gets out of every link.
- **Assessment:** rubric on the trade chain — three links minimum, both sides named at every link,
  and one honest sentence on which link is most likely to fall through.
- **Unplugged:** fully unplugged; optional device use only to look up one tool they named.

## Lesson 4 — finance-cupcakes-stickers ($5)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Atoms vs. Bits Margin Game" and "Two Prices, One Product" (the stretch
  panel); FRAMEWORKS "Leverage = input × multiplier = output."
- **Why a teacher buys this:** it is a unit-rate lesson disguised as a fundraiser, so a math teacher
  and a CTE teacher can both justify it. The table does the teaching; there's nothing to prep but
  copies.
- **Activity:** teams plan the same school-fair fundraiser twice — bake something physical vs. sell a
  design that gets printed — and compute cost-per-one at 10, 100, and 1,000 units from a given
  hypothetical cost card, then choose which one to actually run to hit a target raise.
- **Assessment:** completed cost-per-one table plus a one-paragraph pick that quotes two numbers from
  the table; rubric rewards the student who notices the two columns cross somewhere.
- **Unplugged:** paper cost cards and a calculator; no internet needed.

## Lesson 5 — finance-extra-lives ($5)
- **Corpus source:** FRAMEWORKS "Side projects as risk containers" (real risk, no real downside) and
  "Second- and third-order effects"; SEEDS "Find the Arb" supplies the timing idea used in the
  stretch question.
- **Why a teacher buys this:** risk normally arrives as an insurance worksheet nobody finishes. This
  turns it into a ranking game with a rule card at the end, and it front-loads the probability
  standard a 7th-grade math team is looking for.
- **Activity:** students rank six hypothetical money decisions by *what happens if this goes wrong*,
  set a max they'd risk on each, then flip a card that drops their cushion from five lives to one and
  re-rank — the point is watching their own limits move.
- **Assessment:** a three-rule personal risk card, each rule followed by the reason and one decision
  it would have changed; rubric scores the reasoning, not the caution.
- **Unplugged:** card flips and dice only.

## Lesson 6 — finance-find-the-gap ($6, capstone)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Find the Arb"; FRAMEWORKS "The arb trade / knowledge arbitrage" and
  "Forms of alpha"; VOICE quote 24 ("figuring what you could build for cheap and then sell at a
  higher price than it cost you to build").
- **Why a teacher buys this:** it is the end-of-unit performance task the pacing guide demands, with
  a rubric that forces students to reuse every earlier lesson — so the teacher gets a defensible
  summative grade without writing one.
- **Activity:** teams trade through three rounds of a hypothetical snack-market sim (posted prices,
  round numbers, travel cost per trip), then find and pitch one real *non-money* gap in their own
  school world — a skill the yearbook club needs, a slot nobody's using in the schedule — with a
  cost-per-one estimate and a stated max they'd risk.
- **Assessment:** 90-second pitch plus a one-page plan; the rubric requires one number from L1 (what
  it drains), one from L4 (cost to make one more), and the risk line from L5, or it isn't complete.
- **Unplugged:** the whole sim is paper money and price cards; pitches are live, no slides required.
- **School-appropriateness note for the teacher guide:** the snack sim stays hypothetical and the
  real-world half of the task is deliberately steered to non-money gaps, so no product tells students
  to run a business in the hallway.
