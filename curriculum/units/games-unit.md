# Unit: Game Building & Creative Tech — "Future Play" (6 lessons, grades 6–8)
Bundle target: bundle-games-line ($12–15, not yet in catalog). Every lesson: 45 min, no-prep,
unplugged variant, $4–6 (L1 free). Scope maps to CSTA 6-8 (2-AP-10 pseudocode/flowcharts, 2-AP-11
variables, 2-AP-13 decomposition, 2-AP-15/17 feedback and testing, 2-AP-16 remix with attribution,
2-AP-18 collaborative project timelines), ISTE 1.4 Innovative Designer, and NCAS Media Arts
(codes marked verify): what makes it fun → the core loop → progression → reskin → playtest → jam.
Sits beside, not on top of, the existing games products: this unit owns fun, loops, progression,
remixing, playtesting and shipping; games-balance-math still owns the ratio math and
games-design-doc still owns the long-form design document.

## Unit thesis
After six lessons a student can take the vague sentence "I want to make a game" and hand another kid
something playable before the bell. They can say out loud why a game they quit was boring and point
at the exact minute it happened; write the one action a player will repeat a thousand times as a
four-card cycle and fix the card that makes rep fifty a chore; build a progression path where the
next unlock is the thing the player already wants; separate a game's rules from its skin well enough
to remix a familiar game into a new one that a stranger can play from the spec alone; watch someone
play their game without saying a single word and log where the player hesitated; and then ship a
finished, played, fun-scored game inside one timed jam. The deliverable is not a design document. It
is a game somebody played.

## Unit through-line
**Make → Watch → Cut.** Every lesson ends with a player who isn't the designer touching the thing,
and one change made because of what that player did — and in every rubric, fun is scored before
correctness (FRAMEWORKS "Fun first, education second"; VOICE stance 16, "fun should be top priority
really"). The cut is the hard half: taste is deciding what to delete. Continuity motif: the fun line
graph from L1 reappears as a strip along the bottom of later worksheets so students keep plotting
the same curve.

## Lesson 1 — games-fun-audit (FREE lead magnet, $0)
- **Corpus source:** FRAMEWORKS "Fun first, education second" and "Taste is the new moat" (knowing
  what's good and cutting the slop); VOICE stance 16.
- **Why a teacher buys this:** a free game-design lesson that requires no consoles, no accounts, and
  no game any student needs to already own — it runs on a paper mini-game in the packet. It also
  gives the teacher the vocabulary the rest of a game unit needs, on day one, for nothing.
- **Activity:** students play the provided two-minute paper mini-game (or recall a game they quit),
  plot a "fun line" across five one-minute marks, circle the exact dip, and write the single rule
  change that would fix it.
- **Assessment:** the fun-line graph plus a one-paragraph argument for the change with two reasons
  drawn from the graph; exit ticket.
- **Unplugged:** paper mini-game included; no devices at any point.

## Lesson 2 — games-core-loop ($4)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Fun-First Game Jam" (the loop is the jam's building block); FRAMEWORKS
  "Templates as creative leverage" (a producer never starts from scratch).
- **Why a teacher buys this:** it converts "core gameplay loop" from a phrase in a textbook into four
  index cards a 12-year-old can hold, and it's the cleanest unplugged way to hit the CSTA
  pseudocode and decomposition standards in one period.
- **Activity:** teams write their loop as a four-card cycle — what you do, what happens, what you
  get, why you go again — then physically play ten reps with paper and dice, mark the rep where it
  got boring, change exactly one card, and replay.
- **Assessment:** the four loop cards plus the before/after boredom rep numbers and a sentence
  explaining which card was the problem; rubric.
- **Unplugged:** cards and dice only.

## Lesson 3 — games-skill-tree ($4)
- **Corpus source:** FRAMEWORKS "Skills = XP (build your AI like a game character)"; VOICE quote 4
  (the video-game character and the ability tree) — the same analogy Phi uses for AI skills, pointed
  back at games where it started.
- **Why a teacher buys this:** progression design is what students most want to make and least know
  how to plan. This is also the honest way to teach variables unplugged — HP, XP and coins *are*
  named variables, so the CSTA code isn't a stretch.
- **Activity:** students design a three-branch skill tree where every unlock has an XP cost and
  changes how the loop from L2 feels, then a partner plays five paper levels of it and marks which
  unlock they wanted most and which one was a trap.
- **Assessment:** the tree map, the partner's playtest marks, and one rebalance the designer makes
  because of them; rubric requires the designer to name the variable each unlock changes.
- **Unplugged:** paper tree and dice; no coding required (optional extension for classes with tools).

## Lesson 4 — games-same-rules-new-skin ($5)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Remix 2048, Music Edition"; FRAMEWORKS "Templates as creative leverage"
  and "The Cook and the Chef" (this is the cook move, done on purpose).
- **Why a teacher buys this:** the blank page is where game units stall. A remix gives every student
  a working rule set on minute one, and the "a stranger can play it from your spec" test makes the
  writing standard real instead of decorative.
- **Activity:** teams take a simple public rule set (three-in-a-row, match-the-pairs, tag) and write a
  one-page remix spec — same rules, new theme, new win condition, art direction in three lines —
  then hand it to another team who must play it from the spec without asking a single question.
- **Assessment:** the spec, scored by whether the other team played it question-free, plus an
  attribution line naming the original rules they built on (the CSTA 2-AP-16 habit); rubric.
- **Unplugged:** all originals and public rule sets — no branded games, characters, or logos anywhere
  in the product or the student examples.

## Lesson 5 — games-hands-off-controller ($5)
- **Corpus source:** FRAMEWORKS "Taste is the new moat" and "Shots on goal, not hard work";
  VOICE stance 19 ("what is the new moat? it's like...taste?").
- **Why a teacher buys this:** the feedback-and-revision standards are the hardest to get evidence
  for. The silent-playtest log produces that evidence automatically, and the no-talking rule is the
  most reliably fun ten minutes in the whole unit.
- **Activity:** students run three silent rounds of another student playing their level — the designer
  may not speak — logging every place the player hesitates, then making one cut and one fix between
  rounds.
- **Assessment:** playtest log with three hesitation points, the cut list, and a "what I thought vs.
  what they actually did" paragraph; rubric gives the most credit for the deletion.
- **Unplugged:** paper level from L2–L4; nothing else needed.

## Lesson 6 — games-fun-first-jam ($6, capstone)
- **Corpus source:** SEEDS "Fun-First Game Jam" and "The 1% Streak Tracker" (the take-home);
  FRAMEWORKS "Fun first, education second," "The sea of unfinished beats" (finishing is the scarce
  skill), and "The 1% streak."
- **Why a teacher buys this:** one class period, every student ships, and the teacher gets a
  peer-scored rubric plus a 30-day take-home streak card that keeps the unit alive after it ends.
  It's also the last-week-of-the-quarter lesson that looks like a party and grades like a project.
- **Activity:** timed teams build one playable paper game using the unit's tools — loop cards from
  L2, a small skill tree from L3, a skin spec from L4 — and it must be played start to finish by
  another team before the bell, with that team scoring fun first.
- **Assessment:** jam rubric in two passes — player teams score fun, then the teacher scores
  completeness against the loop/progression/spec artifacts; plus the 1% streak card, a 30-day
  one-rep-a-day creative log students take home and graph.
- **Unplugged:** the whole jam is paper, dice, and index cards; device teams may build the same game
  in a browser tool as an extension, never a requirement.
