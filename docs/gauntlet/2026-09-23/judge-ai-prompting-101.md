## Verdict

This is a strong free lesson, and the core idea is a real winner. The literal genie carries the concept all the way through: the Worst Genie warm-up, the three improv moves, the partner "break it" round, and the "hand over everything after your first five words" check are the kind of thing a teacher tells colleagues about. The teacher lines are quotable ("page 2 is where you do your damage," "Today we stop needing luck"). The what-ifs are real, and a teacher can run it on paper that morning. It is not yet a five-star lesson, for three reasons:

1. **The assessed exit ticket has a design flaw.** The planted false claim ("photo … in 2009") shares one sentence with a claim the key also accepts, so a student can pick the right sentence for the wrong reason. The accepted claim ("the biggest black holes … including ours") is also arguable.
2. **Several bar rules are broken.** The break-it swap has no turn order (rule 14). The exit ticket is worded three different ways across the worksheet, slide and plan (rule 18), and the slide version renders as broken bullets. The write-in boxes are unruled and smaller than the key's own exemplars (rule 15).
3. **The teacher script has three slips a sharp buyer will catch:**
   - The lawyer/contract analogy is a second metaphor aimed at adults.
   - A "forty pages of NASA history" punchline lands before students have seen Amara's wish.
   - A slide says "see the last sentence" about a prompt that is no longer on screen.

None of this is hard to fix. Fix it and this is the freebie that sells the bundle.

## Top 5 moves

1. **Fix the black-hole genie answer and its key** (worksheet :106, teacher guide :80, slides :67). Split the sentence so the false claim stands alone, and reword the galaxy claim so it is true beyond dispute: `…Scientists took the first-ever photo of a black hole in 2009. Giant black holes sit in the middle of most big galaxies, including ours.` Then update the key's "Also accept" list to quote the new sentence. Right now a student who flags the galaxy clause gets credit on the same sentence as the error.
2. **Make the exit ticket one item everywhere, and cut its writing load.** On the worksheet (:107), replace the Exit #2 prompt with `Underline the sentence you would double-check. Where would you check it? Name a real source, like a textbook, a science website, or a librarian:` and one ruled line. Copy #1 and #2 word for word onto slide 10 as two `verbatim: true` items, with no fake indented sub-bullets. In the plan (:118), replace the paraphrase with "students answer Exit Ticket #1 and #2 exactly as printed." Underlining instead of copying saves about 15 handwritten words in a 6-minute block that is currently over budget.
3. **Give the break-it round a turn order and a tick rule** (worksheet :88, plan :109, slide :43). Use the worksheet rewrite in W4 below: tick each part the wish has, twist it out loud, tick Genie test only if the twist fails, and the partner whose first name comes first in ABC order twists first.
4. **Make the write-in space honest.**
   - Page 1 boxes: put 4 ruled lines at 24px inside each box (96px). The exemplars run 155–182 characters, which is 3.4–4.0 handwritten lines.
   - Exit #1: 4 ruled lines, up from a 72px empty box, because its exemplar is 182 characters.
   - Paying for it: the Exit #2 underline change gives back one line on page 2. Tighten the "Be honest" callout's padding if you need more room.
5. **Remove the second analogy and the out-of-order punchline from the script.**
   - Plan :82/:85 and slide notes :28: drop "Lawyers … contracts … clauses." Use "You give it rules." and "Every genie-proof wish has three parts."
   - Plan :101 and slide notes :33: replace "forty pages of NASA history" with "a pile of junk to dig through later."
   - Slide :36: "See the last sentence" becomes "Remember how that prompt ended."
